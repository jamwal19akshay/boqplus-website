This guide explains what a comparative statement is, how to read it, and what past published statements can tell a bidder. The information here is as of 6 November 2026.

## What a Comparative Statement Shows in a Tender

### When it appears

After the financial bids are opened, a comparative statement lists the qualified bidders' prices side by side. Only qualified bidders appear in it. Because the prices sit side by side, you can compare bidders directly.

### Item-rate tenders

In an item-rate tender, the statement shows each bidder's rate and amount for each BOQ item, and the total. If you are not sure which format a tender uses, read [item rate vs percentage rate tender](/blog/item-rate-vs-percentage-rate-tender) first.

### Percentage tenders

In a percentage tender, the statement shows each bidder's quoted percentage and the resulting total.

### How L1, L2 and L3 are ranked

The statement ranks bidders as L1, L2, L3 and so on, from the lowest total. For what these labels mean in practice, see [L1 tender meaning](/blog/what-is-l1-in-tender).

## How to Read a Comparative Statement, Step by Step

1. Check whether the tender is item rate or percentage. This tells you what each bidder's entry contains.
2. Look at the totals and the ranking. L1 is the bidder with the lowest total, then L2, then L3, and so on.
3. For an item-rate tender, go through the BOQ items one by one. Compare each bidder's rate and amount for the same item.
4. For a percentage tender, compare the quoted percentages and the resulting totals.
5. If you are also learning how to fill in your own BOQ, [how to fill a BOQ for an e-tender](/blog/how-to-fill-boq-government-e-tender) covers that side.

## Where to Find Comparative Statements

Many GePNIC-based state portals publish comparative statements in the tender's results or status section. They appear after the financial bids are opened. Look for a tender similar to yours in that section of the portal where it was published.

## What Past Statements Tell a Bidder

Reading past comparative statements shows how other contractors priced similar work. Three things are worth looking at:

- The spread between bidders.
- How far below or above the estimate the winning bid was.
- Which items had the widest range of rates.

The third point is useful when you price your own BOQ. Items with a wide range of rates are the ones where you may want to check your own quotes most carefully.

## A Practical Check Before You Price

1. Find the results for a past tender of similar type and location.
2. Note the spread between bidders on the main items, not only on the total.
3. Compare the winning bid with the estimate, where the statement gives both.
4. Look at the items with the widest range of rates and check your own material and labour quotes for them.
5. Price your BOQ using your own costs. Use the past statement to see the range, not to set your rate.

## Caveats to Keep in Mind

Past results describe the past. They are not a prediction of any future tender. Use a comparative statement as one input alongside your own costs.
